# Square — Q4 resolution through hardware-to-transaction-fee pivot

Square's original revenue model was hardware sales — sell the white card reader to merchants, recognize per-unit revenue. The model worked operationally and produced revenue, but as Q3 resolved (merchants kept using the device) and the company looked at Q4 ("will customers pay enough to build a business?"), the hardware-sales model capped the TAM and the unit economics.

The pivot to transaction-fee pricing (a percentage of every transaction the device processed) changed the Q4 math fundamentally:

- **Aligned incentives:** Square made more when the merchant made more. The merchant's success became Square's success.
- **Unit economics:** CAC stayed flat, LTV grew proportionally with merchant size and longevity. The $500M revenue path became visible.
- **Pricing power:** Square could compete on transparent flat-rate pricing against incumbent merchant processors' opaque tiered pricing — a category reframe.

The Q4 resolution wasn't "we have revenue" (they had hardware revenue from day one). It was "we have a path to $500M with positive unit economics" — a stricter test that the hardware model couldn't pass.

## What the founder did

- Measured unit economics at scale with hardware-sales model; found the ceiling.
- Modeled the transaction-fee pricing alternative against the same merchant cohort.
- Pivoted pricing despite operational momentum on the hardware-sales side.
- Used transparent flat-rate pricing as a category-reframe positioning against incumbents.

## What they refused

- Refused to keep hardware-sales as the primary revenue model.
- Refused to compete on hardware features (the reader was iconic, but the moat was the pricing model).
- Refused complex tiered pricing — kept the flat-rate visibility that incumbents couldn't match without rewriting their economics.

## How this applies to your situation

If you're at Q4 with revenue but weak unit economics, Square is the case to match against. The lesson: revenue is not Q4 resolution. The Q4 criterion (Sequoia, verbatim) is "paying customers who feel they get great value" *and* "chart a path to $500M in revenue." Revenue without unit-economics positivity at scale doesn't clear the bar.

The pricing-pivot pattern is common at Q4: the v1 pricing model often caps the TAM. The Q4 work is modeling the alternative pricing and verifying it produces the $500M path with positive unit economics. Operational momentum on the v1 pricing is irrelevant if the model can't get to Q4.
